Dear NABPR colleagues,

I am working with a team developing an innovative new tool for Bible study. We call it the [Contexticon of New Testament Language](https://contexticon.com/). Honestly, there is nothing like it. Unlike a dictionary or lexicon, the Contexticon describes how New Testament words were used in ancient literature as well as in the New Testament, and it shows the actual contexts in which the words appear. Viewing how words were used gives students a unique experience of immersion into the world of the New Testament and the nuances of its language.

The Contexticon ([contexticon.com](https://contexticon.com/)) is an interactive online tool. It is like a museum with stunning displays, texts, and ancient artifacts and art. Users can toggle between Greek and English, but study can be done completely in English.

The Contexticon is based on the principle that words do not have inherent meanings but acquire meaning by their usage in various contexts: hence, “The Contexticon.” At a time when fewer programs are requiring the study of Greek and many students prefer an interactive online program to reading books, the Contexticon can effectively immerse students in Greek language and culture. You can tour the site with free access for 30 days by registering your e-mail and a password. There is a short video to get you started navigating its range screens and broad context essays. Choose to study either a word or a passage of scripture, KJV, NRSV, or Greek.

After decades of development, we are in the process of inviting universities and seminaries to secure a site license so that the Contexticon can be accessed by anyone with a university or seminary ID, on or off campus. For the first ten libraries the annual subscription rate is $1500 (with a one-time set up fee of $750); after that the rate will rise to $2000 a year.

One professor assigns her students to read the broad context essay on πτωχός and compare the use of the term in the NT with the strikingly different attitudes toward the poor in Greco-Roman and Jewish sources. Another professor divides the class into groups to report on specific passages (like the Prologue to John, the Beatitudes, or 1 Corinthians 13), and the students each choose a key term in the passage to work on. If the faculty will use the Contexticon in classes, it will certainly justify a site license.

Please let me know if you are interested in exploring this resource further.

Sincerely,
